L6@P0: 1.1. Berkovich spaces and some history.
L7@P0: 1.1.1. Naive non-archimedean analytic spaces. Since non-archimedean complete real
L8@P0: valued fields (e.g. Qp) were discovered in the beginning of the century, it was very
L9@P0: natural to try to develop a theory of analytic spaces over such a field k analogously
L10@P0: to the theory of complex analytic spaces. At least, one can naturally define analytic
L11@P0: functions on a subset V of the naive affine line A1
L12@P0: k = k as the convergent power
L13@P0: series on V . This allowed to introduce some naive k-analytic spaces, but the theory
L14@P0: was not reach enough. Actually, a global theory of such varieties does not make too
L15@P0: much sense because the topology of k is totaly disconnected. In particular, locally
L16@P0: analytic (and even constant) functions do not have to be globally analytic.
L17@P0: 1.1.2. Rigid geometry. In his study of elliptic curves with bad reduction over k,
L18@P0: Tate discovered in 1960ies that these curves admit a natural uniformization by
L19@P0: Gm. The latter was given as an abstract isomorphism of groups k
L20@P0: ×/qZf→E(k), and
L21@P0: even such expert as Grothendieck doubted at first that this is not an accidental
L22@P0: brute force isomorphism. Tate suspected, however, that his isomorphism can be
L23@P0: interpreted as an analytic one, and he had to develop a good global theory of
L24@P0: non-archimedean analytic spaces to make this rigorous. This research resulted in
L25@P0: Tate’s definition of rigid geometry, whose starting idea was to simply forbid all bad
L26@P0: open coverings (responsible for disconnectedness) and to shrink the set of analytic
L27@P0: functions accordingly. As a result, one obtains a good theory of sheaves of analytic
L28@P0: functions, but the underlying topological spaces have to be replaced with certain
L29@P0: topologized (or Grothendieck) categories, also called G-topological spaces.
L30@P0: 1.1.3. Berkovich spaces. More recently, few other approaches to ”right” non-archimedean geometry were discovered: Raynaud’s theory of formal models, Berkovich’
L31@P0: analytic geometry and Huber’s adic geometry. They all allow to define (nearly)
L32@P0: the same categories of k-analytic spaces, but provide the analogs of rigid spaces
L33@P0: with additional structures invisible in the classical Tate’s theory. Also, they extend
L34@P0: the category of rigid spaces in different directions. Here we only discuss Berkovich’
L35@P0: theory, which was developed in [Ber1] and [Ber2]. In this theory classical rigid
L36@P0: spaces are saturated with many new points (analogs of non-closed points of algebraic
L37@P0: varieties), and the obtained spaces are honest topological spaces. In addition,
L38@P0: the obtained topological spaces are rather nice (locally pathwise connected, for
L39@P0: example).

L41@P1: Now, let us list few interesting features of Berkovich theory that distinguish
L42@P1: it from all other approaches. First, one can work with all positive real numbers
L43@P1: almost as well as with the values of |k
L44@P1: ×|. In particular, one can study rings of
L45@P1: power series with radii of convergence linearly independent of |k
L46@P1: ×|. The latter
L47@P1: fact allows to include the case of a trivially valued k into the theory, and the
L48@P1: theory of such k-analytic fields was already applied to classical problems of algebraic
L49@P1: geometry. Another interesting feature is that in a similar fashion one can develop
L50@P1: (an equivalent form of) the usual theory of complex analytic spaces. Moreover, one
L51@P1: can define Berkovich spaces that include both archimedean and non-archimedean
L52@P1: worlds, for example, the affine line over (Z, | |∞).
