In classical uniformization theory over the field of complex numbers, one shows
that each non-singular projective curve can be analytically represented as a
quotient Q/T with an open subset Qc IP! and a subgroup 'c PGL(2,C) acting
discontinuously on Q. Similarly, each abelian variety can be represented as an
analytic torus which, from the multiplicative point of view, is defined as a quotient
(€*)9/T by a discrete subgroup I c(C*), free of rank g. Over a non-Archimedean
ground field k, the situation is quite different. The curves and abelian varieties
which admit a good uniformization as above constitute only a small open part in
the corresponding moduli spaces; see [M1] and [M2]. On the other hand, there
are curves and abelian varieties admitting no non-trivial uniformization at all.
This behavior is related to the phenomenon of good reduction, which has no
classical counterpart. In general, good uniformization and good reduction occur in
a mixed way. It is for this reason that uniformization theory is substantially more
complicated in the non-Archimedean case.

The basic tools in uniformization theory over discretely valued fields are the
stable reduction theorem of Deligne-Mumford [DM] and the semi-abelian
reduction theorem of Grothendieck [SGA7]. In order to explain these results,
consider a discrete valuation ring R, and let k be its field of fractions. Let A be an
abelian variety over k, and denote by its Néron model [N, R1]. Then the
theorem of Grothendieck says that (modulo finite separable extension of the
ground field) the identity component of the special fibre /, of «/ is semi-abelian;

Cis a smooth geometrically connected projective curve of genus 22 over k, one
can consider its minimal model @ over R. (See [Ab] or [Li] for the existence of a
regular model and [Sh] for the minimality.) The result of Deligne-Mumford
asserts (again, modulo finite extension of the ground field) that the special fibre @,
of @ has only ordinary double points as singularities. Both results are more or less
equivalent. In fact, the proof of the stable reduction theorem in [DM] uses the
semi-abelian reduction of the Jacobian J of C and the fact that the “identity
component” of the Néron model of J represents the functor Pic®°(€/SpecR); see

The objective of the present article and the article [IT] is to give direct analytic
constructions for the varieties @ and /°. Thereby we obtain a purely analytic
approach to the uniformization of curves and abelian varieties, which works for all
complete non-Archimedean ground fields. In the present article, where we are
concerned with the case of curves, we prove the following analytic version of the
stable reduction theorem:

The curve C can be viewed as a formal analytic variety with associated reduction
a: C—>€ such that € has at most ordinary double points as singularities and such that
each non-singular rational component of C meets the other components in at least 3
points.

The proof is mainly resolution of singularities for arbitrary reductions
n:C—€. As a key tool, we use a refined analysis of the formal fibres of 7; in
particular, the description of peripheries (2.4), the genus formula (4.6), and the
behavior of divisors defining line bundles of finite order (5.3). A different approach
was proposed by van der Put [P].

In [I] we will consider abelian varieties. Using our results on curves, we will
prove an analytic version of the semi-abelian reduction theorem, first for Jacobians
and then for abelian varieties. Also the uniformization of abelian varieties will be
worked out in detail. Finally we mention that our methods are not restricted to the
discussion of uniformization problems. They can also be applied to obtain
information on the existence of minimal models and Néron models. This will be
elaborated on in a further article; in particular, we will show how to deduce the
algebraic version of the stable reduction or the semi-abelian reduction theorem
from the corresponding analytic version.

Using the blowing-down techniques of Sect. 3, the problem of constructing stable
